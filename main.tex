While visualization tools like Kanban boards facilitate process 
monitoring, they lack the capacity for adaptive guidance. To address 
this limitation and the scalability crisis of human expert feedback, 
this study aims to enhance the quality of pedagogical feedback generated 
by Large Language Models (LLMs) to support student Self-Regulated Learning 
(SRL). The enhancement focuses on three core functions: feedback, 
motivation, and appreciation. A comparative experimental method was 
employed without fine-tuning, evaluating various Context Engineering 
schemes enriched with Learning Analytics (LA) on two models: Llama 3.1 8B 
and Llama 3.3 70B. Evaluation was quantitative (BERTScore, BARTScore, 
LLM-as-a-Judge) and qualitatively validated by educational psychology 
experts.

Results indicate that the effectiveness of the enhancement significantly 
depends on the model size. The smaller model (8B) achieved the highest 
performance increase with complex context (ReAct + LA), suggesting a 
reliance on rich, structured guidance to compensate for its internal 
limitations. Conversely, the larger model (70B), despite having a 
superior Baseline performance, showed degraded performance when given 
overly complex context. The best-performing model (Llama 8B with ReAct + LA) 
was validated by human experts as “Usable with minor revision”. 
The study concludes that LA-enriched context engineering is an 
effective, practical, and computationally efficient strategy for 
improving pedagogical feedback, particularly for smaller LLMs.

\textit{Self-Regulated Learning} (SRL)---encompassing
Zimmerman's cyclical phases of forethought, performance,
and self-reflection---is strongly linked to academic
achievement \cite{liu2025srl, zimmerman2009selfregulation},
yet many students lack the skills to apply it effectively
\cite{xiao2019relationships, chitra2022srl}. Personalized
expert feedback is the most effective scaffold for SRL
development \cite{10.3389/fpsyg.2022.1027266, Bock14032024},
but faces a scalability crisis as rising class sizes limit
lecturer--student interaction
\cite{schaffer2017automating, pardo2019using}.

\textit{Large Language Models} (LLMs) offer a scalable
complement, yet their feedback tends to be generic,
overly generous, and process-agnostic rather than
guiding self-regulation
\cite{Usher2025GenAIAssessments,
Maurya2025UnifyingAITutorEvaluation}. This study addresses
these limitations through \textit{context engineering}---the
systematic enrichment of LLM prompts with structured
information---without model fine-tuning. Specifically,
\textit{Learning Analytics} (LA) signals derived from a
Kanban-based platform (card movements, completion timestamps,
stuck indicators) are injected into the prompt to enable
personalized, data-driven pedagogical feedback across three
dimensions: \textbf{feedback}, \textbf{motivational support},
and \textbf{appreciative support}
\cite{strickroth2022kanban, 10.3389/fpsyg.2022.1027266}.

To guide this investigation, three research questions are
addressed:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{RQ1:} To what extent can context engineering
    combined with Learning Analytics improve LLM pedagogical
    feedback quality compared to a structured Baseline prompt?

    \item \textbf{RQ2:} How do human experts perceive the
    quality, relevance, and usability of feedback generated by
    the best-performing context engineering scheme?

    \item \textbf{RQ3:} To what extent does model size
    (8B vs.\ 70B) influence the effectiveness of context
    engineering for pedagogical feedback generation?
\end{enumerate}

The main contributions are: (1) a comparative evaluation of
four LA-enriched context engineering schemes on two
open-source LLMs of contrasting scales; and (2) an empirical
finding that model size fundamentally determines optimal
context complexity, validated through automated metrics
(BERTScore, BARTScore, LLM-as-a-Judge) and expert assessment.

\subsection{LLM Feedback Alignment via Fine-Tuning}

Alignment training---RLHF \cite{ouyang2022traininglanguagemodelsfollow}
and Direct Preference Optimization
(DPO) \cite{woodrow2025dpo_feedback}---has been shown to
improve LLM feedback quality beyond what parameter scaling
achieves: InstructGPT (1.3B, RLHF) outperforms GPT-3 (175B),
and a DPO-tuned Llama~3.1~8B surpasses GPT-4o in preference
and tone positivity in educational grading tasks. However,
both approaches require substantial labeled data and
computational resources that limit applicability in
resource-constrained academic settings.

\subsection{Prompt Engineering and Learning Analytics}

Without retraining, prompt quality is the primary lever for
LLM feedback improvement: Jacobsen and Weber
\cite{jacobsen2025promises} show that only high-quality
prompts reliably produce outputs that surpass novice teacher
feedback, though affective dimensions remain inconsistent.
Enriching prompts with learning analytics further advances
this direction---Li and Ma
\cite{li2025designaipoweredtoolselfregulation} demonstrate
that LA-informed context engines (LACE, KCE) yield
curriculum-aligned SRL feedback in programming education,
while Strickroth et al.\ \cite{strickroth2022kanban} confirm
that Kanban process traces are both pedagogically meaningful
and practically extractable (teacher SUS~92). Existing work,
however, evaluates prompts in isolation or within a single
domain and model scale, leaving open the question of how
context complexity and model size jointly determine feedback
quality across multiple SRL-relevant dimensions.

\subsection{Theoretical Framework}\label{sec:framework}

To formally characterize the role of context engineering 
in LLM inference, this study adopts the lens of implicit 
Bayesian inference \cite{xie2022explanation}. This 
formulation provides a principled explanation of how 
enriching the prompt—with SRL rubrics and learning 
analytics signals—shapes the model's internal belief 
about the student's learning state, thereby directing the 
generated feedback toward pedagogically appropriate 
responses.

\subsubsection{Variable Definitions}

Let $M$ denote the LLM used for inference. The input 
to $M$ consists of two components:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Context Engineering} 
    $C = (c_1, c_2, c_3, c_4, c_5)$, comprising five 
    components: role and persona definition ($c_1$), 
    internal logic and assessment rubric ($c_2$), SRL 
    phase mapping ($c_3$), assessment prompt ($c_4$), 
    and output format specification ($c_5$).

    \item \textbf{Student Data} $D = (K, A)$, where 
    $K = \{\kappa_1, \kappa_2, \ldots, \kappa_m\}$ is 
    the set of $m$ Kanban board cards with attributes 
    $\kappa_j = (\textit{title}_j, \textit{status}_j, 
    t_j^{\text{created}}, t_j^{\text{moved}}, 
    \textit{label}_j)$, and $A = \varphi(K)$ is the 
    learning analytics vector derived from $K$ through 
    a transformation function $\varphi$ that computes 
    descriptive indicators such as completion rate, 
    stuck rate, and velocity.
\end{enumerate}

The complete prompt provided to the model is the 
concatenation:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:prompt}
    \textit{Prompt} = [C,\; D] = 
    [c_1, c_2, c_3, c_4, c_5,\; K,\; \varphi(K)]
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Inference Formulation}

The model $M$ generates a response 
$y = (y_1, y_2, \ldots, y_T)$ as a sequence of $T$ 
tokens. At each step, the model implicitly marginalizes 
over a set of latent concepts $\Theta$ learned during 
pretraining, where each $\theta \in \Theta$ represents 
a student learning pattern (e.g., on-track, struggling, 
procrastinating):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:marginal}
    p_M(y_t \mid C, D, y_{<t}) = \int_{\theta \in \Theta} 
    p(y_t \mid \theta, C, y_{<t}) \cdot 
    p(\theta \mid C, D, y_{<t}) \; d\theta
\end{equation}

\noindent Here, $p(\theta \mid C, D, y_{<t})$ is the 
model's implicit posterior belief about the student's 
learning pattern. The context engineering $C$ forms a 
prior expectation by defining assessment criteria and 
SRL phases, while the student data $D$ provides 
observational evidence for updating that belief. 
The final response is obtained by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:generation}
    y^* = \arg\max_{y} \; p_M(y \mid C, D)
\end{equation}

This response $y^*$ constitutes the pedagogical 
feedback covering three functions---feedback, 
motivation, and appreciation---which is evaluated 
as described in Section~\ref{sec:eval}.

\subsection{Context Engineering and Prompt Variations}
\label{sec:method}

The feedback improvement approach rests on three 
interdependent pillars: context engineering, 
learning analytics integration, and systematic 
prompt variation experiments.

The first pillar is the design of a theoretically 
grounded \textbf{Baseline prompt}, structured into 
five logical components—Role Definition, Internal 
Logic, SRL Phase Mapping, Assessment Prompt, and 
Input/Output Format—to fulfill specific pedagogical 
objectives, including adaptive guidance and 
self-efficacy support. Prompt quality was validated 
using the theory-driven prompt manual of Jacobsen 
and Weber \cite{jacobsen2025promises}, where this 
study's prompt scored 13 out of 16, classifying it 
as high quality. The Baseline prompt structure is 
illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:baseline_prompt_structure}.

The third pillar is a systematic experiment across

\subsection{Evaluation Framework}\label{sec:eval}

Evaluation compares $y^*$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:generation})
against expert ground truth $y_{\text{ref}}$ through
three automated quantitative metrics and qualitative
expert validation.

% -----------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{BERTScore}

% ── ORIGINAL BERTScore (3 equations trimmed to 1-sentence + F1 eq) ───────
% BERTScore computes token-level cosine similarity between contextual
% embeddings of y* and y_ref; R_BERT = coverage of y_ref by y*,
% P_BERT = semantic grounding of y* in y_ref, F1 = harmonic mean.
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

BERTScore \cite{zhang2020bertscore} uses contextual
BERT embeddings to compute token-level Precision
($P_{\text{BERT}}$), Recall ($R_{\text{BERT}}$), and
F1 between $y^*$ and $y_{\text{ref}}$, capturing
semantic similarity beyond lexical overlap:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bert_f1}
    F_{\text{BERT}} = 2\,
    \frac{P_{\text{BERT}} \cdot R_{\text{BERT}}}
         {P_{\text{BERT}} + R_{\text{BERT}}}
\end{equation}

% -----------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{BARTScore}

% ── ORIGINAL BARTScore prose (trimmed for conference format) ─────────────
% BARTScore \cite{yuan2021bartscore} frames text
% evaluation as a generation problem: a high-quality
% response should be assigned high probability by a
% well-trained sequence-to-sequence model. It employs
% a pretrained BART (mBART) model to compute the
% weighted log-likelihood of generating the target
% sequence conditioned on a source sequence. This
% allows BARTScore to assess fluency, factual
% consistency, and paraphrase quality without retraining.
%
% Following the notation of the inference formulation,
% the target is the model-generated response $y^*$
% from Eq.~\eqref{eq:generation}, and the source is
% the expert ground truth $y_{\text{ref}}$. Let
% $\phi$ denote the parameters of the BART model.
% BARTScore is computed as:
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

BARTScore \cite{yuan2021bartscore} evaluates $y^*$
against $y_{\text{ref}}$ by computing the weighted
log-likelihood of $y^*$ under a pretrained BART
model conditioned on $y_{\text{ref}}$, assessing
fluency and semantic coverage without retraining.
Let $\phi$ denote the BART model parameters:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bartscore}
    \text{BARTScore}(y^* \mid y_{\text{ref}}) = 
    \sum_{t=1}^{|y^*|} w_t \log p_\phi
    \!\left(y^*_t \mid y^*_{<t},\, 
    y_{\text{ref}}\right)
\end{equation}

% ── ORIGINAL BARTScore post-formula prose (trimmed) ──────────────────────
% \noindent where $p_\phi(y^*_t \mid y^*_{<t},
% y_{\text{ref}})$ is the conditional probability
% of generating token $y^*_t$ given all previously
% generated tokens $y^*_{<t}$ and the reference
% $y_{\text{ref}}$, and $w_t$ is a per-token weight
% (set to 1 for uniform averaging). A higher
% (less negative) BARTScore indicates that the
% generated response is more probable under the
% BART model given the expert reference, reflecting
% better semantic coverage and generation quality.
% Both directions $\text{BARTScore}(y^* \mid y_{\text{ref}})$
% and $\text{BARTScore}(y_{\text{ref}} \mid y^*)$ are
% computed and aggregated as F1, Precision, and Recall.
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\noindent where $w_t{=}1$ for uniform averaging.
A less negative score indicates better semantic
coverage. Both directions are computed and
aggregated as Precision, Recall, and F1.

\subsubsection{LLM-as-a-Judge}

% ── ORIGINAL LLM-as-a-Judge opening prose (trimmed) ──────────────────────
% The LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm
% \cite{koutcheme2024openfeedback} addresses the
% bottleneck of costly human evaluation and the limited
% correlation of traditional metrics with human judgment
% on open-ended tasks. A capable LLM (GPT-4o) acts as
% an automated evaluator by comparing the generated
% response against the expert reference and assigning
% binary quality scores per pedagogical dimension.
% Studies show that models of this scale achieve
% moderate-to-high agreement with expert human raters,
% making them viable proxies for large-scale
% pedagogical assessment.
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

GPT-4o \cite{koutcheme2024openfeedback} acts as an
automated evaluator, assigning binary quality labels
per pedagogical dimension by comparing $y^*$ against
$y_{\text{ref}}$, achieving moderate-to-high agreement
with expert human raters.

% ── ORIGINAL Algorithm block (trimmed for space) ──────────────────────────
% For each data point i ∈ {1,...,N=40} and each dimension d ∈ D:
%   e^(i)_d = f_phi(I_d, A^(i), y_ref^(i), y*^(i)) ∈ {0,1}       [eq:llm_judge]
%   selected via greedy decoding over P_phi(e | Prompt(...))        [eq:llm_judge_decoding]
% Since expert labels ê^(i)_d = 1, Recall R_d = (1/N) sum e^(i)_d
% Precision P_d = TP_d/(TP_d+FP_d), F1_d = harmonic mean
% Macro-avg: P̄ = R̄ = F̄1 = mean over d ∈ D                        [eq:judge_macro]
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

For each data point $i \in \{1,\dots,N{=}40\}$ and
dimension $d \in \mathcal{D}$, the judge returns
a binary label $e^{(i)}_d \in \{0,1\}$ by comparing
$y^{*(i)}$ against $y^{(i)}_{\text{ref}}$ using
rubric $\mathcal{I}_d$ and analytics context
$A^{(i)}$. Since expert labels are $\hat{e}^{(i)}_d=1$
by construction, per-dimension Recall simplifies to
the mean binary score; Precision and F1 are computed
accordingly. The reported scores are macro-averaged
over all three dimensions $\mathcal{D}$.

% ── ORIGINAL Prompt Template listing (removed for space; kept for reference) ─
% \paragraph{Prompt Template.}
% The judge prompt is structured as follows,
% where placeholders are filled per data point:
%
% \begin{lstlisting}[
%   basicstyle=\ttfamily\scriptsize,
%   breaklines=true,
%   frame=single,
%   label=lst:judge_prompt,
%   caption={LLM-as-a-Judge prompt template used in this study.}]
% # Note: Expert score is always 1 by definition.
% Assign a binary label (0 or 1) for each
% dimension listed below.
%
% DIMENSIONS & DEFINITIONS:
% {dims_def}
%
% Kanban Summary (optional):
% {kanban_brief}
%
% === EXPERT TEXT (Reference) ===
% {expert_feedback}
%
% === LLM TEXT (Candidate) ===
% {llm_text}
% \end{lstlisting}
%
% \noindent The placeholder \texttt{\{dims\_def\}}
% maps to $\mathcal{I}_d$; \texttt{\{kanban\_brief\}}
% maps to the analytics summary $A^{(i)}$;
% \texttt{\{expert\_feedback\}} to $y^{(i)}_{\text{ref}}$;
% and \texttt{\{llm\_text\}} to $y^{*(i)}$,
% establishing a direct correspondence with the
% inference framework of
% Eqs.~\eqref{eq:prompt}--\eqref{eq:generation}.
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The prompt supplies four fields corresponding to
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:prompt}--\eqref{eq:generation}:
dimension definitions $\mathcal{I}_d$, the analytics
summary $A^{(i)}$, the expert reference
$y^{(i)}_{\text{ref}}$, and the candidate response
$y^{*(i)}$.

results are treated as exploratory findings.

\section{Results and Discussion}\label{sec:results}

This section presents the experimental results and 
analysis across three evaluation dimensions---semantic 
alignment, pedagogical relevance, and qualitative 
expert assessment---and discusses the findings in 
relation to the three research questions introduced 
in Section~\ref{sec:introduction}.

% -----------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Semantic Alignment: BERTScore and BARTScore}

Table~\ref{tab:quant_results} reports the BERTScore
and BARTScore F1 scores for all eight experimental
configurations. For the 8B model, both metrics
indicate a consistent and monotonic improvement as
context complexity increases: the ReAct\,+\,LA
variant achieves the highest BERTScore F1
($F_{\text{BERT}} = 0.6663$) and BARTScore F1
($\text{BARTScore} = -11.40$), representing gains
of $+0.0055$ and $+0.32$ respectively over the
Baseline. For the 70B model, the trend is reversed:
the Material List variant achieves the best
performance on both metrics
($F_{\text{BERT}} = 0.6710$;
$\text{BARTScore} = -11.61$),
while the ReAct\,+\,LA variant yields no consistent
improvement over the Baseline.

While the absolute magnitude of these improvements 
is modest, this is consistent with the nature of 
BERTScore and BARTScore: both metrics measure 
token-level semantic proximity to the reference 
$y_{\text{ref}}$, and expert-written feedback 
exhibits idiomatic variation that limits the 
ceiling of embedding-based similarity regardless 
of output quality. The more informative signal 
therefore lies in the consistency of the direction 
of improvement, particularly the steady gain 
observed across all three metrics for the 8B model 
under increasing context complexity.

\subsection{Pedagogical Relevance: LLM-as-a-Judge}

Table~\ref{tab:quant_results} also reports the
LLM-as-a-Judge F1 for all variants. This metric
reveals a much larger performance differential than
the embedding-based metrics, particularly for the
8B model. The Baseline 8B achieves an F1 of only
0.5371, indicating that without structured context
the model satisfies fewer than half of the
pedagogical criteria on average. The ReAct\,+\,LA
variant raises this to 0.6121, a gain of
$+0.0750$---more than ten times the magnitude of
the corresponding BERTScore gain. For the 70B
model, the Baseline already achieves
F1\,=\,0.7271, and the best variant (Material
List, F1\,=\,0.7571) adds only a modest
$+0.0300$.

The consistently high Precision values (1.0000 
for most configurations) reflect the binary 
scoring design described in 
Section~\ref{sec:eval}: when the model satisfies 
a criterion, it virtually always does so 
correctly relative to the expert reference. 
The primary driver of F1 variance is therefore 
Recall---the proportion of expert-defined 
criteria actually addressed---which increases 
substantially for the 8B model under richer 
context (from 0.4567 to 0.5217) but remains 
near its ceiling for the 70B model (0.6417 to 
0.6600 at best). This divergence suggests that 
the 70B model's superior pretraining already 
endows it with broad pedagogical criterion 
coverage, leaving limited headroom for 
context-driven improvement.

\subsection{Effect of Model Size on Baseline 
Capacity}

Table~\ref{tab:baseline_comparison} summarizes 
the Baseline F1 scores across all three metrics. 
The 70B model outperforms the 8B model on every 
measure, with the most pronounced gap appearing 
in LLM-as-a-Judge F1 (0.7271 vs. 0.5371, a 
difference of 0.1900). In contrast, the 
BERTScore gap is narrow (0.6687 vs. 0.6608, 
$\Delta = 0.0079$), suggesting that at the 
surface-level vocabulary of the generated 
feedback, both models produce reasonably similar 
text. The substantially larger LLM-as-a-Judge 
gap confirms that the 70B model's advantage 
lies primarily in its capacity for pedagogically 
structured and criterion-satisfying response 
generation---a quality not captured by 
embedding-based metrics alone.

\subsection{Discussion}

The results collectively address the three 
research questions as follows.

\textbf{RQ1} (effect of context engineering 
with LA): The integration of learning analytics 
consistently improves feedback quality for the 
8B model across all three metrics, with the 
ReAct\,+\,LA variant achieving the highest 
quantitative scores. This confirms that 
LA-enriched context engineering is an effective, 
fine-tuning-free strategy for improving 
pedagogical feedback quality. The improvement 
pattern is consistent with the Bayesian 
interpretation in Eq.~\eqref{eq:marginal}: 
richer context $C$ and student data $D$ sharpen 
the model's posterior belief $p(\theta \mid C, 
D, y_{<t})$ about the student's learning 
pattern, directing generation toward more 
criterion-satisfying outputs.

\textbf{RQ2} (expert perception): The 
overall verdict of ``Usable with minor revision'' 
validates the practical feasibility of the 
approach as a complementary scaffolding system. 
The average expert scores across all three 
dimensions ($\bar{s} = 3.87/5.00$) indicate 
adequate pedagogical quality, with the primary 
revision targets being contextual specificity 
(F3), empathetic tone (M5), and language 
naturalness (A2, A5). These improvements do 
not require architectural changes and can be 
addressed through targeted prompt refinement.

\textbf{RQ3} (model size): Model size 
fundamentally determines the optimal context 
complexity. The 8B model benefits monotonically 
from richer context---consistent with the 
hypothesis that smaller models compensate for 
limited internal capacity by relying on 
explicit structured guidance 
\cite{tang2025fewshot, pawlik2025choice}. 
Conversely, the 70B model achieves its best 
performance with minimal additional context 
(Material List), and its performance degrades 
under the ReAct\,+\,LA framework. A plausible 
explanation is that the highly prescriptive 
ReAct loop over-constrains the 70B model's 
generation distribution, forcing it to mimic 
the step-by-step, descriptive style 
characteristic of the 8B model rather than 
leveraging its superior language generation 
capacity. This also accounts for the qualitative 
observation that 70B generates more 
linguistically varied feedback at Baseline, 
while 8B tends to be repetitive and fails to 
clearly separate appreciation from critique.

A cross-cutting finding is the divergence 
between metric sensitivities: BERTScore 
improvements are small (max $\Delta F1 = 
0.0055$) while LLM-as-a-Judge improvements 
are substantially larger (max $\Delta F1 = 
0.0750$ for 8B). This suggests that 
pedagogical criterion satisfaction is more 
sensitive to context engineering than 
surface-level semantic similarity, and that 
LLM-as-a-Judge is a more discriminative 
metric for this task. Finally, the finding 
that a prompt scoring 13/16 on the 
theory-driven quality manual 
\cite{jacobsen2025promises} does not 
guarantee optimal output---especially for 
the 70B model---demonstrates that prompt 
quality scores are a necessary but not 
sufficient condition for performance, and 
must be interpreted alongside model capacity 
and context complexity.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

This study investigated context engineering enriched with
learning analytics (LA) as a fine-tuning-free strategy for
improving the pedagogical feedback quality of open-source
LLMs in a Kanban-based self-regulated learning platform.
Four prompt variants---Baseline, Material List, LA, and
ReAct\,+\,LA---were evaluated on two models of contrasting
scales (Llama 3.1 8B and Llama 3.3 70B) using a tri-metric
framework combining BERTScore, BARTScore, LLM-as-a-Judge,
and qualitative expert validation.

Regarding \textbf{RQ1}, the integration of learning analytics
into the prompt context consistently improved feedback quality
for the 8B model across all three quantitative metrics.
The ReAct\,+\,LA variant achieved the highest BERTScore F1
($F_{\text{BERT}} = 0.6663$, $\Delta{+0.0055}$ over Baseline)
and BARTScore F1 ($\text{BARTScore} = -11.40$,
$\Delta{+0.32}$), while LLM-as-a-Judge showed the largest
improvement ($\Delta F1 = 0.0750$). The wider gap observed
in LLM-as-a-Judge relative to embedding-based metrics
confirms that pedagogical criterion satisfaction is
substantially more sensitive to context enrichment than
surface-level semantic similarity---underscoring
LLM-as-a-Judge as the more discriminative evaluation
instrument for this task.

Regarding \textbf{RQ3}, model size fundamentally
determines the optimal context complexity, and larger does
not imply more context-receptive. The 70B model achieved
its best quantitative performance with the lightweight
Material List variant ($F_{\text{BERT}} = 0.6710$), while
the ReAct\,+\,LA framework produced no consistent
improvement. A plausible explanation is that the
prescriptive Thought--Action--Observation loop
over-constrains the 70B model's generation distribution,
suppressing the linguistically rich and varied output the
model otherwise produces at Baseline. The 8B model, by
contrast, compensates for limited internal capacity by
leveraging structured guidance---consistent with the
theoretical interpretation that richer context $C$ sharpens
the model's posterior belief $p(\theta \mid C, D, y_{<t})$
in Eq.~\eqref{eq:marginal}.

Regarding \textbf{RQ2}, qualitative expert evaluation of
the best-performing configuration (8B ReAct\,+\,LA)
returned an aggregate verdict of ``\textit{Usable with
minor revision}'' ($\bar{s} = 3.87/5.00$), confirming the
practical feasibility of the approach as a complementary
scaffolding tool. Feedback quality was assessed positively
across three pedagogical dimensions, with targeted revision
recommendations centered on contextual specificity (F3),
empathetic tone (M5), and language naturalness (A2, A5)---
all of which are addressable through prompt refinement
rather than architectural modification. Taken together,
these findings establish context engineering enriched with
learning analytics as an effective, resource-efficient, and
practically viable strategy for improving LLM pedagogical
feedback quality in scalable educational settings.

Several directions emerge from these findings. Understanding
why the 70B model degrades under the ReAct\,+\,LA
framework warrants further investigation, with preference
optimization (DPO) or alternative agentic strategies as
candidate solutions. Future studies should replicate these
experiments on real student Kanban interaction logs and
expand the expert panel to enable statistically robust
inter-rater agreement analysis. Prompt personalization
for tone and empathy---particularly for the indicators
flagged by experts (M5, A2, A5)---and evaluation of models
with stronger target-language proficiency remain open
avenues for improvement.
